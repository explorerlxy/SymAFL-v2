## Motivation

Fuzzers without an effective test-case pre-screening mechanism must execute every generated candidate before determining its testing value from runtime feedback. After an execution completes, the fuzzer examines the resulting feedback—such as newly observed program behaviors, coverage changes, abnormal states, or detected failures—and decides whether the test case should be retained or used in subsequent scheduling. However, a substantial fraction of generated candidates produce no observable testing gain and are eventually discarded. Although these executions do not advance the testing campaign, they still consume the full cost of running the target program. This execution-after-generation workflow can therefore waste considerable computational, memory, and I/O resources. The problem becomes particularly severe when target executions are intrinsically expensive, heavily instrumented with runtime checks, or performed through emulation, where redundant executions directly constrain test throughput and overall testing efficiency. Prior studies on fuzzing acceleration are likewise motivated by the observation that many generated tests do not contribute new coverage or other useful feedback, despite incurring execution or instrumentation costs.

An ideal pre-screening mechanism must satisfy two fundamental requirements: it must **screen accurately** and **screen efficiently**. Accurate screening requires the mechanism to retain candidates that may produce testing gains while rejecting a substantial proportion of candidates that would not contribute useful feedback. These two objectives are asymmetric. Incorrectly admitting a non-contributing candidate merely reduces the amount of execution cost saved, whereas incorrectly rejecting a gainful candidate may prevent the fuzzer from discovering new behaviors or failures. Consequently, overall prediction accuracy alone is insufficient; a screening mechanism should be evaluated separately by its gainful-candidate retention rate and its non-contributing-candidate rejection rate.

Efficient screening requires more than a low per-input decision latency. Let (C_e) denote the average cost of a complete target execution, (C_s) the per-candidate screening cost, (C_b) the total cost of constructing and maintaining the screening mechanism, (N) the number of processed candidates, and (a) the fraction of candidates admitted for execution. Without screening, the average cost per candidate is approximately (C_e). With screening, it becomes

$$
C_s + aC_e + \frac{C_b}{N}.
$$

Screening can improve candidate-processing throughput only when

$$
C_s + \frac{C_b}{N} < (1-a)C_e.
$$

In other words, the execution cost avoided by rejecting non-contributing candidates must exceed the construction, maintenance, and query costs introduced by the screening mechanism. Moreover, throughput improvement alone is not sufficient: a mechanism that indiscriminately rejects candidates can appear fast while destroying testing effectiveness. A useful screening mechanism must therefore improve the number of candidates processed per unit time while preserving, or preferably increasing, the amount of testing gain achieved under the same time and resource budget.

Existing techniques address parts of this problem, but they leave important limitations along the two dimensions of screening accuracy and screening cost. One class performs prediction without executing the candidate on the target. FuzzGuard, for example, trains a deep-learning model to predict whether an input can reach a specified target location in directed fuzzing. This design can avoid target execution entirely for predicted-unreachable inputs, but it depends on target-specific labeled data, model training and online updating, and probabilistic generalization to previously unseen inputs and execution paths. Furthermore, its prediction objective is target reachability rather than the more general question of whether a candidate will produce testing gain under a dynamically evolving fuzzing state.

Another broad class uses a lighter-weight execution as a screening stage before the final expensive execution. Prefix-Guided Execution runs an initial execution prefix and uses the observed prefix behavior to determine whether the remaining execution should continue. UnTracer and HeXcite initially execute candidates with reduced coverage-tracing overhead and invoke more expensive tracing only for selected inputs. SAND executes every candidate on a normally built target and invokes the sanitizer-enabled target only when the lightweight execution exhibits an interesting execution pattern.

These execution-assisted approaches provide useful ways to reduce particular components of fuzzing cost, but they have two inherent limitations as general pre-screening mechanisms. First, every generated candidate must still incur some target-execution cost. Prefix-based methods save only the execution suffix, while selective tracing and sanitization primarily save the additional cost of instrumentation or runtime checking. Their maximum benefit is therefore bounded when the dominant cost lies in the target program itself—for example, in computation-, memory-, or I/O-intensive programs—or in an execution environment such as emulation that remains necessary even for the lightweight run.

Second, the lightweight execution observes only a restricted approximation of the feedback available from the final complete execution. A prefix does not observe behaviors that occur only in the omitted suffix. Reduced tracing captures only the coverage properties represented by its selected instrumentation scheme. A normally built execution does not directly observe failures that require sanitizer checks. Consequently, these mechanisms must trade the amount of execution they save against the completeness of the evidence used to make the screening decision. PGE explicitly embodies a trade-off between prefix length and the ability to retain coverage-increasing tests, while UnTracer and HeXcite focus on reducing coverage-tracing overhead and SAND focuses on selectively invoking sanitizer-enabled executions. Their published evaluations demonstrate that these designs can be effective for their respective objectives, but their lightweight execution stages do not constitute a general, zero-execution-cost predictor of testing gain.

Therefore, an important gap remains in high-execution-cost fuzzing: there is still a need for a lightweight and reliable pre-screening mechanism that can assess generated test cases before any expensive target execution, reject a substantial proportion of non-contributing candidates, retain candidates that may advance the testing campaign, and introduce sufficiently low construction and decision overhead to produce a net improvement in testing throughput and effectiveness.
